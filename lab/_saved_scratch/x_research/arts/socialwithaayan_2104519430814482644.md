Part 1 · Build the studio
## 01. Code - the video is a program
Opus 5.5 can't make a video file. It writes text.
So every AI launch video you've seen is a program. Something else turns that program into frames:
1. Opus writes one web page with one function, seek(t). It draws the exact frame for any moment in time.
1. A headless browser (a browser with no window) calls it once per frame and saves a screenshot.
1. ffmpeg, a free video tool, joins the screenshots and the sound into an MP4.
Nothing runs on a timer. Same code, same video, every time.
That's the unlock. A change isn't a trip back to a studio. It's one line and a re-render.

## 03. Rules - the file that sets the standard
Claude Code reads a file called CLAUDE.md at the start of every session. Write your standard once, and every video inherits it:
```
Make the same video in two more formats from the same timeline.js: square 1080x1080 and wide 1920x1080. Reframe the layout for each format, do not crop. Keep the same sound. Export one contact sheet per format and check it before rendering.
```
Two lines do most of the work.
"Every time lives in timeline.js, written in beats." Picture and sound read the same file, so nothing drifts. Moving a moment is a one-number edit.
"Never redraw UI." This is the line between a launch video and a showreel. A showreel sells the animator. A launch video sells your product. If the screens are fake, so is the video.

## 05. Kit - real sound, measured
This is the upgrade nobody talks about.
The first build of this course had Opus synthesize the music and the clicks in Python. It worked. But code-made beeps are the fastest way to make a good video feel cheap.
Real recordings sound expensive. So build a kit once, and every video reuses it:
```
Make clip.mp4: a 20-beat screen demo of Tallyo's reminders, the product in kit/. Follow CLAUDE.md and LOOK.md, and use the tempo in kit/AUDIO.md.

Film it like a screen recording with a smart camera. The whole real dashboard sits on the canvas as one big card, and the camera zooms to wherever the cursor works. The cursor scales with the zoom.
Beats 0-3: wide on the dashboard. The cursor moves to "Reminders" in the sidebar and clicks on beat 3.
Beats 3-5: the reminders page pushes in from the right. The camera zooms onto the timeline card, which is empty.
Beat 6: the cursor flips the real "Reminders on" switch from off to on, with a click.
Beats 7-13: the timeline fills in from the real page, one step every two beats, each with a soft ding.
Beats 14-17: the camera pulls back to the whole page, and the caption "Reminders go out for you." rises in at the top left, with "for you" as the serif accent.
Beats 18-20: hold, then the cursor leaves the frame.

Use the real "before" screenshots in kit/shots (the switch off, the empty steps). Show me three stills (beats 3, 6 and 16) before you render the full clip.
```

It picked "Downtown Rags": 120 beats per minute, a clean grid, and a breakdown that drops at 64.06 seconds. At 120 BPM, one beat is exactly half a second, so every timing in this course reads like a clock.
Three things it caught that you'd never hear:
1. Sounds peak late. The whoosh hits its loudest at 712 milliseconds. Start it on the moment and it lands late. Start it 712 ms early.
1. The MP3 hides a 25 ms delay. Convert it to WAV once, or every beat lands a hair behind.
1. The typing file is 23 seconds long, with one loud key in the middle. It flagged it before anything was built on it.
And the honest part: it measured the music. It never heard it. Listen before you build on it.
Mixkit's free license covers commercial use. Your own licensed song works too. Drop it in and ask for the same measurements.
Part 2 · Five scenes, five techniques
Here's where most people lose. They ask for the whole film at once.
When a 30-second film comes back wrong, you can't tell which part broke. So you re-roll the whole thing and hope.
Build each scene as its own clip instead, in its own folder, with its own prompt. One technique per clip. One thing to judge. When all five are right, one last prompt joins them.

- It started the words half a beat early, so "You" lands exactly on the music's first downbeat.
- It chose the brief over the rules when they clashed. LOOK.md says one word per beat, but the brief gave five words two beats. So four words come in fast and "money." gets its own beat. Then it said so in its report.

## 08. Morph - one shape, never cut
The most bookmarked launch-style video of the month ran on one idea: a shape that never cuts. A button becomes a page. A page becomes a pill. A pill becomes the next screen.
Here it is with real screens inside the shape:
```
# Look: Tallyo launch

- Canvas: warm white #F7F8F6, with a soft 5% tint of the accent behind whatever is in focus. No dark scenes.
- Type: Manrope 800 from kit/fonts for headlines, letter-spacing -0.045em. One accent word per headline in Instrument Serif italic (Google Fonts), in Tallyo green #0B8F63.
- UI: real crops on white cards, radius 20, 1px #E6E9E7 border, shadow 0 12px 32px rgba(21,32,27,.08).
- Text rises out of a mask line, one word per beat. Nothing fades in and nothing blurs in.
- Things change shape into the next thing instead of cutting: a dot grows into a button, a button stretches into a card.
- Green means good news. #C24A1B is only for "late".
- Banned: 3D, glows, particles, gradients on UI, crossfades, any hold longer than a beat and a half.
```

49 minutes. $5.60. The longest clip, because morphing real UI is harder than morphing drawn UI:
- The crops had the page background baked in, so they'd show as gray rectangles on a moving shape. It re-shot every element from the real pages with transparent backgrounds.
- The shape clipped its own contents. Shrinking onto the pill overshot by 17 pixels and cut "Sent" to "Sen". It firmed up the spring on the big shrinks.
- Color changes read as fades, which LOOK.md bans. So on the click, a white circle now grows out of the cursor and floods the button. That's how a color change stays a shape change.

That's the behavior you want. It would rather show you a flaw than fake a fix.
The technique: one element, the whole time. Only its size, corner radius, fill and contents change. If you can point at a moment where one thing disappears and another appears, it's a cut, not a morph.

## 12. Check - make it watch itself
Opus 5.5 reads images. It has never seen a video it didn't look at.
That's why every prompt in this course ends with a check, and why CLAUDE.md makes it a rule. Two checks do most of the work:
1. The contact sheet. One frame per beat, on one image. It catches layout problems: text too small, things off-center, a scene that holds too long.
1. The pop scan. It compares every frame with its neighbors and flags any frame that jumps far more than the ones around it. That catches the one-frame glitches your eye misses at full speed and your viewers notice on the third loop.
Across the five clips, the checks caught text re-centering in jumps, a badge drawn behind its card, a "£-0.00", a halo that read as a glow, and an encoder that quietly broke a perfect loop.

```
# Launch video rules

## Product
- Every product pixel comes from kit/shots: real screenshots and crops (positions in kit/crops.js). Never redraw UI.
- Need a state the screenshots don't have? Change the real page in kit/site and screenshot it.
- No invented numbers, names or prices. Only what's on the pages.
- kit/ is shared and read-only. Write everything into this folder.

## Engine
- One index.html with window.seek(t). Every frame is a pure function of time: no CSS animations, transitions, timers or requestAnimationFrame, and nothing remembered between frames.
- Every time lives in timeline.js, written in beats. The picture and the sound both read it.
- Springs are closed-form (a formula of time) with a small overshoot. A value that changes target more than once is a sum of springs, one per change.
- The camera is one transform on one container. Eased keyframes, one move at a time, zoom interpolated in log space.

## Render
- 1920x1080, 60 fps. Capture 4 subframes per frame and blend them with ffmpeg tmix for motion blur. Use 8 on any moment where something moves more than 15px a frame, or the blur shows as steps. H.264, yuv420p, AAC.

## Sound
- Music and effects come from kit/audio (real recordings, listed in kit/AUDIO.md). Never synthesize.
- The music starts on a downbeat. Place each effect so its measured peak lands on its event. Loudness -14 LUFS.

## Before you show me
- Render one frame per beat into contact.png, look at it, and fix the three worst problems.
- Scan the finished video for single-frame pops (a frame that jumps far more than its neighbours) and fix them.
- Tell me what you fixed and what you would still change.
```

## What it still gets wrong
1. It can't hear. Every clip said so. It set the mix by the numbers and never listened. You have to.
1. It only checks what you make it check. The contact sheet and the pop scan exist because the rules demand them.
1. Motion blur lies on fast moves. 4 subframes showed steps. 8 still showed steps on the fastest moves. Some clips needed 16. Often the better fix is a slower move.
1. Real data doesn't line up. It won't invent data to make your story tidy. Build your test data so it tells one story.
1. Long runs are slow. 30 to 50 minutes per clip on high. Start them together and walk away.

## Tools
- Mixkit: free music and sound effects, cleared for commercial use. https://mixkit.co
- whatships.com: a catalogue of launch films from X, for finding your three.
- Remotion: make videos in React, if you'd rather use a framework. https://www.remotion.dev
- HyperFrames: HTML to video, built for agents. https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes
This course didn't use a framework. Opus 5.5 wrote its own small engine in every clip, which is what it does when you don't name one. If you want Remotion or HyperFrames, say so in CLAUDE.md.
## The uncomfortable part
The animation was never the hard part of a launch video. It was the expensive part.
Look at what Opus did in this course. It measured 27 songs. It caught a sound kit logging the wrong half of a click. It refused to print "£-0.00". It noticed its own glow on a loop frame. That's craft, and craft is cheap now.
Now look at what it didn't do. It didn't decide the pain was "chasing the money". It didn't pick the payoff. It didn't know the story should end on £0.00.
That part was never going to be automated. It's the part that was always yours.
So the skill that's left isn't motion design. It's knowing your product well enough to write the beats.
