Who gets paid

The AI rules, and the swearing rules

Not monetisable since 16 July 2026

  • Generic, repetitive or template content. Text-to-speech over stock slideshows, cookie-cutter formats.
  • Off-putting or emotionally manipulative content.
  • AI personas on sensitive topics: finance, legal, health and medical. An AI narrator giving money advice gets no ads.

Reported enforcement: mass-AI channels removed in waves, and repeat offenders get a warning, then a 90-day suspension, then removal from the Partner Program.

What keeps us safe

  • A human in every video: your voice, your jokes, your picks, a recurring character, original visuals. Nothing templated.
  • Money channels on finance, law or health need your real voice, not George and not a voice changer. Or we pick high-paying niches that aren't on that list.
  • Swearing: since July 2025 strong swearing is fine anywhere in the video, even the first 7 seconds. Never in titles or thumbnails, not every other word, and no slurs ever.
  • Disclosure: tick "altered or synthetic" when something realistic is AI-made.
Research

What gets clicked, and what gets finished

Nobody has published a study comparing crazy and mild hypotheticals. The evidence on viral video points one way: a premise has to be extreme or outlandish to travel, and it only keeps travelling if the payoff is real. YouTube now ranks mostly on satisfaction, so a video people rate "worth my time" beats a longer one they sat through.

Our rules for every video

  1. Crazy premise, sober payoff. "What if WW3 started tomorrow?" beats "How to prepare for emergencies". The answer inside is real, useful and sourced.
  2. Premise in the first 5 seconds. People watch for the payoff, not the surprise. Say what we're doing, then open one loop.
  3. Packaging first. Title and thumbnail get written before the script. Three titles per video, tested with Test & Compare where YouTube offers it.
  4. A pattern break every 20–40 seconds: a new visual, a joke beat or a silence cut.
  5. Series, not one-offs. Numbered episodes and recurring characters keep people in the session, which is now a big long-form signal.
  6. Close the loop. Every video ends on the thing it promised, then one question for the comments.

Signals YouTube reportedly weighs in 2026

  • Post-watch surveys: "was this worth your time?"
  • Viewers coming back to the channel within 7 days.
  • Shares off the platform.
  • Completion on the right-sized video.
  • Shorts and long-form are ranked separately, so a flop Short doesn't drag the long videos down.

From creator-analytics trackers, not YouTube's own docs, so treat the weights as reported.

Your brain dump, joined up

The show: a god with the attention span of a golden retriever

Logline: a stickman with the powers of a god and zero self-control drops into history's worst moments and tomorrow's craziest hypotheticals, messes about, and somehow you come out knowing how to survive them.

Who he is

  • All-powerful, fully unserious. He can summon anything, pause time, rewind, teleport. He uses it to get a better tan, win arguments and skip queues.
  • He can't die, only get flattened, sooted, bonked, folded or posted through a letterbox.
  • He never talks. You narrate: to him, about him, and sometimes as him ("we'll be fine here"). He answers with faces, gestures and sound effects.
  • Working name: Big Man. Ironic for a stickman, and it's how you already talk about him. Put other names in the ideas box.

Jumping through pages

  • Our signature transition: he grabs the edge of the frame, rips it like paper and steps through into the next scene. Every scene is a page of the story.
  • It lets us jump anywhere (a bunker, a beach, a cockpit, 2030) with no setup scenes, and it's instantly recognisable on a thumbnail and in the first second of a Short.
  • Variations: he peels a scene off like a sticker, folds it into a paper plane, or is stuck halfway through the page with his legs kicking.

The look: a flat cartoon in a real world

  • Real-looking backgrounds, flat bright him. The contrast is the joke. He stays a clean white line with the chrome hat; the world is photographic.
  • He really is there: a contact shadow under his feet, the rock in front of him when he hides behind it, sand kicking up when he runs, water splashing, camera shake when something lands, and a rim light tinted by the scene so he sits in its light.
  • How we build it: backgrounds come out of Higgsfield or Canva as separate layers (the scene, plus the things he can hide behind cut out). I composite him between them with the physics, then add your voice, the effects and the music.

The comedy toolkit

  1. Hubris, smash cut: "we'll be fine here", instant nuke, then him covered in soot, blinking. "Fuck."
  2. The cut-off reaction: cut on the "oh—", mid-word. Never let a reaction finish.
  3. Tom and Jerry reveal: he looks under something, a beat of silence, then a comically large fist sends him flying into a letterbox, legs sticking out.
  4. The music switch: he plugs his phone into the aux, and his song becomes the soundtrack.
  5. Wrong way first: he does the stupid thing, you say the right thing, and the right thing ends up on screen.
  6. Punch-in zooms on his face, freeze frames with a caption, record-scratch rewinds.
  7. No dead air. Nothing over a third of a second unless the silence is the joke.

Set pieces for the WW3 guide

  • The cockpit: in the helicopter to the front line, he leans into the cockpit and plugs into the aux. The pilot stares at him ("what are you even doing up here?"), the track kicks in and carries the next scene.
  • Gunship cam: he calls in air support from a black-and-white thermal gunship view, our own take on the idea.
  • The beach: he's sunbathing on a sun lounger while the landing happens around him, then the tide of history washes over him.
  • The bunker: big man in a bunker, running the war from a gaming chair.
  • The nuke: "we'll be fine here", smash cut, soot, "fuck", then the real "go in, stay in, tune in".
  • The fist: he checks under the car for the tin opener, and the fist sends him through a letterbox.

So it stays monetised and doesn't get claimed

  • No famous songs. "Fortunate Son" would get flagged by Content ID, and the ad money goes to the label or the video gets blocked. We use a licensed library track or an original with that late-60s rock feel, never a sound-alike of the actual song.
  • No game footage, menus or sounds. Referencing a chopper gunner is fine; our gunship cam is drawn by us.
  • Meme sounds: lots of the famous ones are copyrighted clips. We build our own library: bass boom, record scratch, the "oh—" cut, a fake laugh track. Licensed meme packs are the backup.
  • Real history, recreated: no real footage of real deaths. The joke is always on him, never on the people who were there.
  • Cartoon violence only, no gore, and tick the "altered or synthetic" box because the backgrounds look real.

Character bible

The stickman in the chrome hat

A main character who can't die, so the most dangerous stuff on Earth can be a joke while the advice stays real. He never speaks. You narrate, he reacts, and sometimes he ignores you completely.

Look

  • Body: a clean black-line stickman with a round head and dot eyes, drawn with weight and squash-and-stretch so he feels alive.
  • The hat: a chrome conical rice-farmer hat, the only shiny thing in frame. It reflects the scene, including explosions, sunsets and the narrator's disappointment.
  • Faces: blank, determined, panic, a smug grin, and the dead-serious close-up with a dramatic sting.
  • Palette: graphite and turquoise like the channel, with a warning orange for danger.

Troll face warning: the original Trollface is copyrighted and its creator has collected licensing fees and had a game pulled over it. We draw our own smug face instead, which is also better for owning the character.

Rules of his world

  • Looney Tunes physics: he gets flattened and pops back, gets blackened by a blast and blinks, falls off a cliff and waits for gravity to notice.
  • The hat does everything: shield, umbrella, wok, sledge, satellite dish, parachute, begging bowl.
  • He is always a step ahead or a mile behind the narrator, never level. The joke is the gap.
  • Every gag carries a real tip. The joke shows the wrong way, the narrator gives the right one, and the tip ends up on screen as a checklist line.
  • A running bit per episode, such as the one tin of beans he refuses to share.
