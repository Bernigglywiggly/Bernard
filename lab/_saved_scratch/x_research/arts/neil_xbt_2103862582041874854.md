# How to Use Opus 5.5 as Your Marketing Video Director (Full Course)
"Make a dynamic 15-second motion graphics video that shows what an incredible motion designer you are. Go all out."
That prompt, word for word, is behind most of the Opus 5.5 videos on your feed this week. One of them did 981K views. Another did 2 million: the poster spoke to his computer for five minutes, Opus worked for twelve hours, and he woke up to a finished piece.
Then people tried it on their own product and got the same video everyone else got. Centered text. Gradient background. Everything fading in.

The gap is not the model. Every video you saw came from the same Opus 5.5. The gap is what the good ones fed it before the prompt, and what they said after the first render. One builder who reverse-engineered a stack of these put it plainly: "everyone has the same model. the context you give it is what makes it look pro."
This is the manual for the marketing version of that. Not a showreel. A product video, a feature launch, a UI loop for your landing page, a stat card for the announcement post, a repo trailer. Four payloads, the cost math, and the honest section on what "one prompt" actually hides.

## three facts that decide how you run it
1. It writes the video as code, and something has to render it. Opus 5.5 does not output video. It outputs HTML, CSS and a timeline, one frame a pure function of time, and a renderer turns that into an MP4. Without one, you get a rough web page you have to screen-record. With one (HyperFrames or Remotion, both free), every frame is exact, and a change is one edited line and a re-render instead of a redo. The 817K video whose author open-sourced his template says it in four words: "this entire video is code."
2. Effort is the dial, and the default is medium. Anthropic's own guide: Opus 5.5 defaults to medium, and at medium it matches Opus 5 at high on their evaluations. It also says to reserve xhigh and max for work where you have measured a gain. The viral showreels were mostly run at max. For a product video, medium for the storyboard and stills, max only for the final render pass, is where the quality shows and the bill does not.
3. It has a default look, and you have to name it to kill it. Also from Anthropic's guide: asked for frontend work without direction, the model falls back on a few default styles, and a vague instruction like "avoid a generic AI look" just swaps one default for another. What works is naming the specific patterns to avoid. That is why the open-sourced template has a "banned" list, and why the marketing version below has a longer one.
## the cost arithmetic
Opus 5.5 is $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output. Cache reads are $0.20. A twelve-hour unattended run is real money: one builder posted a video captioned "what $2,175 of Opus 5.5 tokens can do."
The other end of the scale is my own receipt. I built a small tool that turns a repo into a launch trailer: Opus 5.5 writes the storyboard, a gate checks every on-screen number against the file line it came from, HyperFrames renders a vertical and a horizontal cut, and Opus reviews one frame per scene and fixes what is wrong. The first real run: seven scenes, 26 seconds, eight model calls, about $1.18 in API-equivalent tokens, on a Max plan so nothing was billed. Rendering took about 30 seconds per cut.
The difference between $1.18 and $2,175 is not quality. It is whether you gave the model a brief and a gate, or an open-ended "go all out" and twelve hours.

## payload 2: the marketing prompt template
The 817K template was written for a UI showreel. This is its marketing cousin. Same bones (inputs, direction, structure, build, gotchas, start), different job. Paste it into Claude Code with the Context Stack in the working folder.
```
1. REFERENCE  One or two videos in the style you want. Name the style ("Linear launch
              video", "Stripe docs motion", "Apple keynote bumper"). Naming a style works
              far better than describing one. Without a reference the model uses its
              default look, and that is why the feed all looks the same.

2. RENDERER   Install HyperFrames (npx hyperframes init <dir> --skill=motion-graphics)
              or Remotion. This is what makes it a video instead of a web page.

3. COMPONENTS A real UI kit (21st.dev or your own design system) so the product UI in
              the video is made of real buttons, cards and inputs. Without it the model
              draws your product from memory: wrong spacing, placeholder boxes, fake
              buttons. Anyone who has used good software feels it in a second.

4. BRAND      Logo as SVG, exact hex colors, font names it can load (Inter and
              JetBrains Mono always resolve; an unusual font will fail the renderer's
              lint). Three to five real screenshots of the product.

5. FACTS      Every number that will appear on screen, with where it came from. "3x
              faster" with no source is how you ship a claim you cannot back.

6. BRIEF      Five lines: who the video is for, the one thing they should remember,
              where it will run (X feed, landing page, YouTube pre-roll), length, and
              the call to action.
```
Two lines in there do most of the work. "Render one still frame per scene first and stop" is the Still-Frame Gate: fixing a storyboard costs seconds, fixing a render costs a re-render. And "every number matches the facts list" is the only thing standing between you and a video that says something your product does not do.
## payload 3: Director Notes
The first render is usually about 80 percent there. The last 20 percent is the difference between a demo and a product video, and it comes from notes. Vague notes ("make it better", "more energy") get random changes. Camera and timing words get exactly the change you asked for. Six that cover most of it:
Give notes one scene at a time, against the still frames, and re-render only that scene. A note that says "in scene 3" costs a fraction of a note that says "overall."
## payload 4: the five marketing videos and what to change
Same template, five jobs. What changes is the structure block and the length.
```
<inputs>
Ask me for: the product name and one-sentence promise; the 3 to 5 screenshots and
the SVG logo in ./brand; the hex palette and fonts; the reference video(s) and the
style name; the 4 to 6 on-screen facts with their sources; where the video runs and
its length; the call to action. Do not start until you have all of them.
</inputs>

<direction>
Match the named reference style. One continuous camera, never a hard cut unless I
ask for one. The product's real UI (from ./brand screenshots and the component kit)
is the hero; it enters once and every scene is a transformation of it, not a new
slide. Brand palette only, one accent. Every number on screen appears exactly as
written in the facts list, with its unit. Copy is short: under 6 words per line, one
line at a time. Springs with at most a tiny overshoot. Finish on the logo and the
call to action, held for 1.5 seconds.

Banned: centered headline over a gradient, everything fading in, particle bursts,
glows, lens flares, stock 3D blobs, italic accent words, numbered 01/02/03 labels,
pill buttons that are not in the kit, any claim not in the facts list, any font not
in the brand list, dead time longer than half a second.
</direction>

<structure>
[LENGTH] seconds at [BPM] BPM. Something changes on every beat.
Hook (0 to 2s): the problem, in the product's own UI, one line of copy.
Turn (2 to 4s): the product's promise, the UI transforms to show it.
Proof (4 to N-3s): one scene per fact, each fact drawn from the UI, not typed over it.
Close (last 3s): logo, call to action, held.
</structure>

<build>
1. One HTML file at [ASPECT] (9:16 for X and Reels, 16:9 for YouTube and the site).
   Every style is a pure function of time inside seek(t): no CSS transitions, no
   timers, no state carried between frames.
2. Real screenshots are placed as images and animated with transforms; never
   redraw the product.
3. Build the timeline on the beat grid and start on a downbeat.
4. Render one still frame per scene FIRST and stop. Do not animate until I approve
   the stills.
5. Then render at 60fps with the renderer's high quality preset. Then pull one frame
   per scene and check: no clipped text, nothing under 28px on the vertical cut,
   nothing hidden under platform UI at the top and bottom of a phone screen, every
   number matches the facts list.
</build>

<start>
Ask me for the inputs. Then show me the scene list on the beat grid, with the fact
each scene carries, before you write any code.
</start>
```
The HyperFrames skill's own rule fits all five: keep it under ten seconds where you can, up to thirty at most, no narration. Marketing video on a feed is watched muted and half-scrolled. The first two seconds are the whole ad.

## the honest version
"One prompt" is a caption, not a workflow. The reverse-engineering post says it directly: their one-prompt attempt "looked mid." The 2M-view video came from five minutes of spoken brief plus twelve hours of unattended work. The context stack is the prompt.
The default look is real and named in Anthropic's docs. If your video has centered text over a gradient with things fading in, you did not give it a reference and a banned list. That is a setup failure, not a model limit.
Max effort is a choice, not a requirement. Anthropic's guide says to reserve max for measured gains. Run the storyboard and stills at medium. Spend max on the final render if the stills earned it.
Motion designers are not cooked. The videos that traveled were made by people who already knew what a good reference was, what "push in" means, and why a hard cut lands on a beat. The model executes taste. It does not supply it.
Your product's numbers are your liability. A model asked to "go all out" will write "3x faster" over your UI because nothing stops it. Put the facts in a list with sources, and check each frame against the list. That check is the only part of this pipeline I would not skip.
Renderers have rules. Fonts must resolve or the lint fails. Terminal text wider than 46 columns is unreadable in a vertical cut. A render is about 30 seconds per 18-second cut on a normal laptop. None of this is hard, all of it is a surprise the first time.
